UNDERSTAND WHAT YOU SEE

Fast and shallow → slow and deep.

01

Recognize the flow

The Froude number compares flow velocity to the speed of long, shallow-water surface waves. Supercritical flow has Fr > 1; subcritical flow has Fr < 1. At Fr = 1, the flow is critical.

Fr₁ = V₁ / √(g y₁)
02

Conserve mass and momentum

The discharge stays the same. With negligible external drag and hydrostatic pressure at the end sections, the momentum balance gives the sequent-depth relation.

Q = B y₁ V₁ = B y₂ V₂
y₂/y₁ = ½[√(1 + 8Fr₁²) − 1]
03

Account for the energy loss

Organized flow energy is transferred into turbulence and ultimately dissipated. Mechanical energy decreases across the jump; total energy is still conserved.

E = y + V²/(2g)
ΔE = (y₂ − y₁)³ / (4 y₁ y₂)

Froude number and jump type

Compare the incoming water speed with the speed of a shallow surface wave, √(gy₁). At Fr₁ above one, even a wave traveling against the current is swept downstream. Choose a flow preset in the experiment to highlight its range below. The boundaries are approximate.

How a stilling basin protects the channel

Water leaving a spillway can scour the riverbed. A stilling basin provides a lined reach where the jump can dissipate mechanical energy before the flow returns to the river. The downstream water level, or tailwater, helps hold the roller in the basin. If the tailwater is too shallow, the jump can sweep downstream. Excess tailwater can submerge it.

Momentum and conjugate depths

Read depth vertically. At fixed discharge per unit width, the shallow upstream state (orange) and deeper downstream state (teal) share the same horizontal coordinate, specific force M. The minimum of the curve marks critical depth. Compare these states with the specific-energy graph above.

Follow the water through one jump

Set the upstream depth to 0.20 m, velocity to 4.5 m/s and width to 1.00 m. Each second, 0.90 m³ of water crosses the upstream section. The same volume must pass downstream each second. As the water gets deeper, it moves more slowly.

How deep? Write q for the flow per metre of channel width. Continuity gives q = yV = 0.90 m²/s. In the momentum balance, the water pressure on each end and the momentum carried through each end combine into the specific force per unit width, M(y) = y²/2 + q²/(gy). Across this short, horizontal jump, M(y₁) = M(y₂). Factoring out the equal-depth solution gives y₁y₂(y₁ + y₂) = 2q²/g. Solving for the deeper state gives the depth ratio above.

Here Fr₁ = 3.21. The model predicts a rise to 0.814 m and a drop in speed to 1.11 m/s. The downstream Froude number is 0.39. The two depths have the same specific force, yet the energy graph shows different specific energies.

Where did the energy go? The churning water dissipates mechanical energy. Specific energy drops from 1.232 m to 0.876 m, a head loss of 0.356 m or 28.9% of the incoming specific energy. Applying a lossless Bernoulli equation here would erase the very loss we want to calculate. These values are model predictions for the stated inputs.

Assumptions, limitations, and dimensional analysis

The model assumes steady incompressible water flow (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²), constant rectangular width, a horizontal bed, hydrostatic end-section pressure, uniform end-section velocities, and negligible bed/sidewall drag over the jump. Inputs describe the flow immediately upstream, not a gate-opening prediction.

Fr₁ > 1 is necessary but does not guarantee that a stationary jump will occur at a particular location. Downstream boundary conditions determine whether a jump is held, swept downstream, or submerged. Here matching tailwater is assumed. An undular jump is represented by surface waves rather than a turbulent roller.

For the ideal model, y₂/y₁ depends only on Fr₁. Real jumps can also depend on Reynolds number, relative roughness, channel aspect ratio, and air entrainment. Dimensional analysis identifies possible dependencies; it does not derive the Bélanger equation or supply experimental correction factors.

The energy graph uses y/y₁ and E/y₁ at the current unit discharge q = y₁V₁. Both states lie on the same specific-energy curve, at different energies. The critical depth minimizes specific energy.
