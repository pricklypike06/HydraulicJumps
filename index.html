UNDERSTAND WHAT YOU SEE

Fast and shallow → slow and deep.

01

Recognize the flow

The Froude number compares flow velocity to the speed of long, shallow-water surface waves. Supercritical flow has Fr > 1; subcritical flow has Fr < 1. At Fr = 1, the flow is critical.

Fr₁ = V₁ / √(g y₁)
02

Conserve mass and momentum

The discharge stays the same. With negligible external drag and hydrostatic pressure at the end sections, the momentum balance gives the sequent-depth relation.

Q = B y₁ V₁ = B y₂ V₂
y₂/y₁ = ½[√(1 + 8Fr₁²) − 1]
03

Account for the energy loss

Organized flow energy is transferred into turbulence and ultimately dissipated. Mechanical energy decreases across the jump; total energy is still conserved.

E = y + V²/(2g)
ΔE = (y₂ − y₁)³ / (4 y₁ y₂)
Assumptions, limitations, and dimensional analysis

The model assumes steady incompressible water flow (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²), constant rectangular width, a horizontal bed, hydrostatic end-section pressure, uniform end-section velocities, and negligible bed/sidewall drag over the jump. Inputs describe the flow immediately upstream, not a gate-opening prediction.

Fr₁ > 1 is necessary but does not guarantee that a stationary jump will occur at a particular location. Downstream boundary conditions determine whether a jump is held, swept downstream, or submerged. Here matching tailwater is assumed. An undular jump is represented by surface waves rather than a turbulent roller.

For the ideal model, y₂/y₁ depends only on Fr₁. Real jumps can also depend on Reynolds number, relative roughness, channel aspect ratio, and air entrainment. Dimensional analysis identifies possible dependencies; it does not derive the Bélanger equation or supply experimental correction factors.

The energy graph uses y/y₁ and E/y₁ at the current unit discharge q = y₁V₁. Both states lie on the same specific-energy curve, at different energies. The critical depth minimizes specific energy.
