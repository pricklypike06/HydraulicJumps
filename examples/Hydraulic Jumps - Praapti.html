01 — FIRST PRINCIPLES

Why the depth must jump

A hydraulic jump is not a wave travelling along — it is a standing discontinuity. Flow arrives supercritical (Fr > 1: faster than a surface wave can travel, so no information can get upstream) and must become subcritical downstream (Fr < 1) because the tailwater, a gate, or a stilling basin demands a greater depth. A smooth transition is impossible — the specific-energy curve forbids it — so nature does it abruptly, in turbulence.

Start with two conservation laws

Continuity (mass, steady flow, rectangular channel of width B): the same discharge passes sections 1 and 2.

Q = B·y₁·V₁ = B·y₂·V₂  ⇒  q = Q/B = y₁V₁ = y₂V₂

Momentum in the streamwise direction, over a short horizontal, frictionless control volume. The only horizontal forces are the hydrostatic pressure forces on the two end sections — friction and weight have no streamwise component worth keeping over so short a length. That is the whole trick: across a jump, momentum (specific force) is conserved, energy is not.

P₁ − P₂ = ρQ(V₂ − V₁)
P = ½·ρgB·y²  (hydrostatic, centroid at y/2)

Combine them → the Bélanger equation

  1. Substitute P₁, P₂ and V₂ = q/y₂ into the momentum balance and divide through by ρgB.
  2. Factor out (y₂ − y₁). One root is the trivial y₂ = y₁ (no jump). Discarding it leaves a quadratic in y₂/y₁.
  3. Solve, and define Fr₁ = V₁/√(g y₁). The density and width cancel completely.
y₂ / y₁ = ½ · ( √(1 + 8·Fr₁²) − 1 )
y₁, y₂ are called sequent (conjugate) depths.

Energy: what the jump costs

Specific energy is E = y + V²/(2g). Because turbulence dissipates energy, E₂ < E₁ always. Substituting the Bélanger result gives a remarkably clean loss formula — note it depends only on the two depths:

ΔE = E₁ − E₂ = (y₂ − y₁)³ / (4·y₁·y₂)   ·   Jump length Lj ≈ 6·(y₂ − y₁)  (empirical)   ·   Efficiency E₂/E₁ falls as Fr₁ rises
Why engineers love (and fear) it: that “lost” energy is exactly the point of a stilling basin below a dam spillway — the jump destroys energy that would otherwise scour the riverbed. But if tailwater is too low, the jump is swept out downstream; too high, and it drowns against the gate and loses its roller. Design is mostly about keeping the jump where you put it.

02 — THEORY + EXPERIMENTAL DATA

Theory is close — but systematically high

In a typical undergraduate flume run (horizontal rectangular channel, sharp sluice gate, depths read with point gauges upstream and well downstream of the roller), the measured sequent depth lands a few percent below Bélanger. That is expected: the derivation ignored bed friction and assumed uniform velocity (momentum correction factor β = 1), hydrostatic pressure, and no air entrainment — all only approximately true, and air entrainment alone inflates a point-gauge reading in the roller zone if you measure too close to the toe.

Sequent-depth ratio vs Froude number

What agrees

Shape and scaling: y₂/y₁ grows almost linearly with Fr₁ at high Fr (asymptotically y₂/y₁ → √2·Fr₁ − ½). A log–log plot is straight — the first hint, used in §3, that one dimensionless group runs the whole show. Energy loss grows even faster, roughly as Fr₁³ at moderate Fr.

What doesn't — and why

Measured y₂ sits 2–4% low and the scatter grows with Fr₁. Causes, in likely order: bed + sidewall friction over the jump length (removes momentum the theory keeps), β > 1 in the highly non-uniform toe region, entrained air biasing depth readings, and scale effects at low Reynolds number. None of these appear in Bélanger — which is precisely the gap dimensional analysis fills.

03 — DIMENSIONAL ANALYSIS → CORRELATIONS

Eight variables in, five groups out

Suppose we refuse to do the momentum derivation and just ask: what can y₂ depend on? For a jump in a rectangular channel the honest list is:

y₂ = f( y₁, V₁, g, ρ, μ, B, ks )
depths [L] · velocity [L T⁻¹] · gravity [L T⁻²] · density [M L⁻³] · viscosity [M L⁻¹ T⁻¹] · width [L] · roughness height [L] — 8 variables in total (y₂ plus the 7 it depends on), 3 fundamental dimensions (M, L, T)

Buckingham Π theorem: 8 − 3 = 5 independent dimensionless groups. Choosing y₁, V₁, ρ as repeating variables:

Π₁ = y₂ / y₁ — the answerΠ₂ = V₁/√(g y₁) = Fr₁Π₃ = ρV₁y₁/μ = Re₁Π₄ = B / y₁Π₅ = ks/y₁
y₂/y₁ = Φ( Fr₁ , Re₁ , B/y₁ , ks/y₁ )

Now the experimental data earn their keep. Plot y₂/y₁ against Fr₁ for runs at different widths, depths and viscosities and — above Re₁ ≈ 10⁵, in wide channels (B/y₁ > ~5) on smooth beds — all points collapse onto one curve. Re, aspect ratio and roughness drop out (to experimental accuracy), leaving a one-variable correlation — and it is the Bélanger curve, recovered without ever writing a force balance:

y₂/y₁ = ½(√(1 + 8 Fr₁²) − 1)   valid: Fr₁ > 1, Re₁ ≳ 10⁵, wide smooth channel

The working correlations engineers actually fit

  1. Friction-corrected Bélanger (fit to the §2 data): (y₂/y₁)exp = 0.968 × (y₂/y₁)Bélanger, R² ≈ 0.999 over 1.7 ≤ Fr₁ ≤ 8.1. The single factor 0.968 absorbs average bed friction for a smooth lab flume — refit it for your channel.
  2. Energy-loss correlation: ΔE/y₁ = ( (y₂/y₁) − 1 )³ / ( 4·(y₂/y₁) ) with y₂/y₁ from above — or, fitted directly, ΔE/E₁ rises from ~0.05 at Fr₁ = 2 to ~0.70 at Fr₁ = 9.
  3. Jump-length correlation (USBR-type): Lj/y₂ is a function of Fr₁ alone, rising from ≈ 4 at Fr₁ = 2 to a plateau ≈ 6.1–6.3 for Fr₁ ≥ 5 — the number you need to size a stilling basin floor.
  4. When the extra Π groups bite: below Re₁ ≈ 10⁵ (small lab flumes!) viscous scale effects lift y₂/y₁ measurably; in narrow channels sidewall friction does the same; on rough beds the 0.968 factor falls further. A correlation quoted without its Fr–Re range is only half a result.

The full circle: first principles (§1) told us the form — momentum conservation forces the Bélanger curve. Experiment (§2) told us the error — a consistent few percent, in a consistent direction. Dimensional analysis (§3) told us why one curve can describe every flume, which extra effects to expect, and how to package the correction as a portable correlation. Theory, data, dimensions — each one checks the other two.
